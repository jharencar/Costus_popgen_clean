\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Study system}

The Neotropical spiral gingers (\textit{Costus}) form a large clade of Central and South American herbaceous perennial understory plants that have diversified into approximately 84 species within the last ~3 million years \citep{vargas_patterns_2020,maas_eighteen_2023}. These species generally lack postzygotic barriers, remaining broadly interfertile \citep{kay_natural_2008}, but commonly display pre-mating barriers such as habitat and pollinator isolation \citep{uckele2024prezygotic,kay2022evolutionary,vargas_patterns_2020}. \textit{Costus allenii} (Jacq.) and \textit{C. villosissimus} (Maas) share pollinators and have overlapping ranges, but display strong ecogeographic isolation, in which interbreeding is limited by divergent adaptation to habitats varying in frequency across geographic space (Figure \ref{fig:figure_1_ecogeo_iso_summary.pdf}; \citealp{chen_experimental_2011}). This ecogeographic isolation is the primary isolating barrier between \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus}\citep{chen_experimental_2011,chen_sexual_2013,chen_ecological_2015}. \citet{chen_ecological_2015} showed that \textit{C. villosissimus} has extremely low survival in \textit{C. allenii}'s perennially wet, dark forest understory habitat, and \textit{C. allenii} cannot survive seasonal drought in \textit{C. villosissimus}'s high-light forest edge habitat \citep{chen_ecological_2015,chen_adaptation_2019}. \textit{Costus villosissimus} avoids drought as a seedling through seed dormancy. As an adult, it achieves drought adaptation through flexible drought-deciduousness enabled by active leaf drop (senescence) in the dry season followed by rapid growth with the onset of the wet season \citep{chen_ecological_2015,harencar_flexible_2022}. Rapid growth comes with a tradeoff in which \textit{C. villosissimus} invests less in defense against herbivory, whereas \textit{C. allenii} grows more slowly but has greater investment in chemical and structural defenses \citep{harencar_growth-defense_2024}). This lower defense investment in \textit{C. villosissimus} aligns with differences in biotic selection pressures between habitats; the primary beetle herbivores of \textit{Costus} require wet habitat, such that there is lower herbivore pressure in the seasonally dry, open habitat of \textit{C. villosissimus} \citep{harencar_growth-defense_2024}. \par

\subsection{Sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing}
\subsubsection{Wild sample collection}
In Central Panama, \textit{Costus allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus} are generally distributed along a precipitation gradient, with \textit{C. villosissimus} primarily growing near forest edges on the seasonally dry southern side of the isthmus and \textit{C. allenii} primarily growing in dark ravines on the perennially wet northern side of the isthmus (\ref{fig:2_recent-ancient_hyb}A.i). Around the center of the seasonality gradient of precipitation, previous research noted potential wild hybrids \citep{chen_experimental_2011}). We collected samples from 38 \textit{Costus allenii} individuals, 23 \textit{C. villosissimus} individuals, and, around the center of the seasonality gradient of precipitation, 12 hybrids. To facilitate tests for introgression, we also collected tissue from 21 individuals of \textit{C. allenii}’s sister species, (\textit{C. bracteatus} (ingroup), from Costa Rica, and from two \textit{C. lasisus} (outgroup) individuals – one from Panama and one from Peru (Figure \ref{fig:2_recent-ancient_hyb}B.ii). 

\subsubsection{Genetic mapping population generation}
We generated a mapping population of F3 hybrids by self-pollinating a wild F1, growing and self-pollinating the resulting F2 seeds in the UCSC greenhouses, and self-pollinating and collecting seed from five F2s to generate 390 F3s. Producing an F3 mapping population in the greenhouse minimizes maternal effects from variable field conditions. We also closely tracked the conditions experienced by groups of F3 individuals, such that a plant's "cohort" captures most of the environmental variability experience by mapping population plants. This means that variance in a trait not captured by our QTL model is less representative of plasticity due to environment and more indicative of numerous contributing genetic interactions and loci of small effect. We collected and extracted DNA from the 390 F3s, the original wild F1 parent, and 21 F2s including the five F2 parents of the F3s. 

\subsubsection{DNA extraction, sequencing, and initial data cleaning}
For all DNA extractions, we used Macherey Nagel Plant II kits (Düren, Germany) on meristematic tissue dried in silica. For the wild collected samples used in population genomic analyses, we generated libraries for low-coverage whole-genome sequencing with Tn5 transposase and tagmentation, then sent them to Fulgent Genetics for paired-end 150 bp read sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq4000. An additional 6 wild samples were sent together with all the mapping population individuals to the DNA Technologies Core at the UC Davis Genome Center for their High Throughput library preparation and sequencing (also Illumina 150pe). 

We inspected FASTQ files with FastQC \citep{andrews_simon_fastqc_2010}, trimmed adapters and low-quality bases with Trimmomatic v0.39 \citep{bolger_trimmomatic_2014}, and removed PCR duplicates using the dedupe function in BBTools v39.01 \citep{bushnell_bbmap_2014}. Next, we mapped reads to the \textit{C. lasius} genome (NCBI accession number GCA\_027563935.1; \citealp{harencar_genome_2023}), using BWA mem version 0.7.15-r1140 \citep{li_fast_2009}. Mapping to the \textit{C. lasius} genome avoids reference bias, as \textit{C. lasius} is sister to the small clade containing \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus}, making it equally related to both focal species. We removed ten \textit{C. allenii} and three \textit{C. villosissimus} from the wild collected samples with outlier low coverage (<0.15x) or low coverage breadth (<10\%), and one outlier F3 sample with lower than 0.05X average coverage across the genome from future analyses. We filtered out unmapped reads, reads smaller than 35 bp, and reads with mapping quality lower than 20. We then calculated mappability with genmap \citep{genmap_Pockrandt_2020}, using 15 bp kmers and a 100 bp max distance for merging regions, and filtered out regions with low mappability (below 0.7). When calling SNPs we applied a base and map quality filter of 20 and a 0.05 MAF filter, removed sites within three base pairs of an indel, and removed sites with coverage depth greater than the 99th percentile.

\subsection{Population genetics of wild plants}
\subsubsection{Recent hybridization}
We visualized patterns of gene flow between \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus} with Principal Component Analysis (PCA). In addition to the filters described above, we applied a 1e-6 p-value SNP calling cutoff and increased base and mapping quality filters to a threshold of 30, and only included sites with at least 1x coverage in at least 80\% of each species and the hybrids. We ran ANGSD to estimate genotype likelihoods with Samtools, inferring the major and minor alleles \citep{korneliussen_angsd:_2014}. We then created the PCA from genotype likelihoods with PCAngsd.  

\subsubsection{Local ancestry assignment}
We mapped local ancestry across the genome for each individual that appeared to be a hybrid from the wild population PCA using Ancestry HMM \citep{corbett2017hidden}. We used sequences from 18 wild \textit{C. villosissimus} and 24 wild \textit{C. allenii} individuals for the parent reference panels. When generating the aHMM input panel from our VCF (with $new_vcf2aHMM.py$; see DRYAD/github repository), we set mean recombination rate to 0.7, a minimum rate of genotype calls for each parent population to three, minimum mean depth for the admixed population to 0.5 (1 read per individual), distance between sites to 5000 bp, and minimum allele frequency difference between populations to 0.7. We ran aHMM with a uniform 0.02 error rate and a single bidirectional hybridization pulse (50\% from each population) at time 0.0001 to account for recent hybridization by disfavoring very small ancestry blocks. We validated parameters by running aHMM on known F1 hybrids and confirming heterozygous calls.  

\subsubsection{Ancient introgression}
To determine whether \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus} experienced ancient introgression in addition to the observed recent hybridization, we removed all individuals appearing as hybrids in the molecular PCA of wild individuals, leaving 28 \textit{C. allenii} and 18 \textit{C. villosissimus} individuals, and conducted ABBA-BABA tests for ancient gene flow \citep{graham_304_2010,durand_testing_2011,soraggi_powerful_2018}. We tested for introgression across the whole genome and each chromosome with our population genetic samples using the -doabbababa2 1 function from the ANGSD package, specifying genome blocks of 5 Mb and a minimum base and mapping quality of 30 \citep{soraggi_powerful_2018}. For ABBA-BABA test ingroup, we used a set of 21 \textit{C. bracteatus} individuals that were collected, extracted, sequenced, and processed together with all the population genetic samples. The outgroup was the reference \textit{C. lasius} genome. To calculate the D-statistic, standard error, and Z-score, we used ANGSD’s R script, ‘estAvgError.R’. For per-chromosome p-values, we used a false discovery rate (FDR) p-value adjustment to account for multiple testing across chromosomes \citep{benjamini_controlling_1995}.

To evaluate whether evidence of introgression might be limited to Panama, we ran another ABBA-BABA test using a different dataset with a \textit{C. villosissimus} individual from Ecuador, which is outside \textit{C. allenii}'s range. For this analysis, we used capture sequencing data from 756 genes from \citet{vargas_patterns_2020}. Because this dataset contains hard genotype calls, we used Dtrios from Dsuite to run the ABBA-BABA test \citep{malinsky_dsuite_2021}. We included one \textit{C. bracteatus} from Costa Rica as the ingroup, one \textit{C. allenii} from Panama, one \textit{C. villosissimus} from Ecuador, and two \textit{C. lasius} from Panama and Peru as the outgroup. The input VCF contained 25,978 variants that were divided into 20 Jackknife blocks (block size = 1297 variants) for calculation of the D-statistic.

\subsubsection{Genome-wide differentiation}
We calculated multiple differentiation and introgression statistics across the genome to characterize patterns and relate them to the genetic basis of habitat isolation trait between \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus}. To characterize genomic differentiation, we generated all sites vcfs (including invariant sites) with bcftools mpileup, applied the same base filters described above in \textit{DNA extraction, sequencing, and initial data cleaning}, and also removed sites with more than 80\% missingness in either species. We then calculated average nucleotide diversity within species (\(\pi\)) and average nucleotide difference between species (\(D_{xy}\)) in 10kb non-overlapping windows across the genome using PIXY \citep{korunes_pixy_2021,weir_estimating_2024}. To characterize introgression, we used a modified D-statistic (\(f_{dM}\)) that is better for sliding windows and depends on the derived allele frequencies across species \citep{martin_evaluating_2015,malinsky_genomic_2015,malinsky_dsuite_2021}. We calculated \(f_{dM}\) in 50 variant windows with 25 variant steps with Dsuite’s Dinvestigate from vcfs filtered as above for quality, coverage and mappability, and to only include biallelic SNPs further than 3 bp from indels and with alternate allele frequencies between 0.05 and 0.95 \citep{malinsky_whole-genome_2018,malinsky_dsuite_2021}. We used two low-coverage \textit{C. lasius} individuals and the \textit{C. lasius} reference genome for the outgroup and 21 low-coverage \textit{C. bracteatus} individuals for the ingroup, along with 21 low-coverage \textit{C. allenii} and 18 low-coverage \textit{C. villosissimus} genomes. No hybrids from the PCA were included.

\subsection{Quantitative trait mapping}
We selected phenotypes to genetically map based on previous work demonstrating a role for those phenotypes in habitat isolation, including traits related to life history, ecophysiology, and herbivore defense.

\subsubsection{Trait measurement}
The primary abiotic factors driving divergent adaptation are moisture and light \citep{chen_ecological_2015}. \textit{Costus villosissimus} has several adaptations for seasonal drought that \textit{C. allenii} lacks, and which cause mortality of \textit{C. allenii} in \textit{C. villosissimus}’ habitat \citep{chen_adaptation_2019}. The first of these adaptations is seed dormancy: \textit{C. villosissimus} seeds are dormant in the dry season whereas \textit{C. allenii} seeds germinate as soon as they reach the soil. Adult \textit{C. villosissimus} is adapted to drought through flexible drought deciduousness; during drought the plants limit water loss through active senescence of leaves (reabsorbing nutrients as leaves die). When rain returns, the plants take advantage a higher light habitat to rapidly grow new leaves \citep{harencar_flexible_2022}. Conversely, \textit{C. allenii} is adapted to perennially wet, darker forest understory habitat and dies without substantial leaf nutrient reabsorption during drought. \textit{Costus allenii} is also unable to take advantage of higher light to rapidly grow new leaves like \textit{C. villosissimus} (Figures \ref{fig:1_ecogeo_iso} and \ref{fig:sup_trait_distributions}). 

Differences in the biotic habitat select for \textit{C. allenii}’s lower investment in growth as it trades off with higher investment in defense against herbivores. \textit{Cotus allenii}’s darker, wetter habitat holds a higher abundance of specialist herbivores. \textit{Costus allenii} has thicker, tougher leaves with greater chemical defense against herbivores. These differences in defense between \textit{C. allenii} and \textit{C. villosissimus} result in specialist herbivores preferring to eat \textit{C. villosissimus} in controlled trials, indicating that they would encounter high levels of herbivore damage in \textit{C. allenii} habitat \citep{harencar_growth-defense_2024}. 

\paragraph{Seed dormancy}
Seed dormancy is an important adaptation in \textit{C. villosissimus} to avoid drought, whereas \textit{C. allenii} has no seed dormancy and seedlings die during drought in \textit{C. villosissimus} habitat \citep{chen_experimental_2011,chen_ecological_2015,chen_adaptation_2019}. To score dormancy in our F3 mapping population, we tracked germination under controlled conditions in Percival seed incubators. Incubators were set to a 12-hour day length with a daytime temperature of 30°C and a nighttime temperature of 24°C. We collected and washed seeds, then placed them on consistently wet cotton. We recorded seed cohort - the batch of seeds harvested and placed on cotton together. Twice a week, we surveyed all seeds for germination, which was determined by radicle emergence. We recorded the number of days from harvest/sowing in the growth chambers to germination as seed dormancy. After cotyledon emergence, we planted seedlings into four-inch pots in Pro mix HP +mycorrhizae and Biofungicide.

\paragraph{Growth rate}
Rapid growth in \textit{C. villosissimus} takes advantage of a higher light environment and enables drought adaptation through flexible drought deciduousness \citep{harencar_flexible_2022}), whereas slower growth in \textit{C. allenii} trades off with greater defense against herbivores in a lower light, higher herbivore pressure habitat. To measure growth rate in the F3 mapping population, we took height measurements when seedlings had at least 3-5 open leaves and again after 3-4 weeks. All seedlings were grown in growth chambers with 12-hour day length and 25°C constant temperature and were fertilized twice (start of week 1 and week 3) by watering evenly with 1 tsp Peter’s Professional NPK 20-20-20 fertilizer in 1 gallon of water. We calculated growth rate in cm per day as the total growth across all stems divided by the number of days between measurements. We evaluated the potential impact of plant cohort (plants that reached 3-5 leaves and started in the growth chamber together) on growth rate by fitting a simple linear mixed model with growth rate as the response and cohort as the dependent variable. We identified three dates with unusual values and removed them from the plotting of trait distributions. We kept these cohorts for trait mapping and accounted for cohort effects with covariates (see “Genetic map construction”). Within ~1 week of the second growth rate measure, we moved plants from the growth chamber into the greenhouse and from four-inch pots to 1-gallon pots. 

\paragraph{Leaf nutrient reabsorption during drought}
Unlike wet forest understory adapted \textit{C. allenii}, adult \textit{C. villosissimus} is adapted to drought through flexible drought deciduousness; during drought the plants limit water loss through active senescence of leaves (reabsorbing leaf nutrients like Nitrogen and pigments like chlorophyll as leaves die). Conversely, \textit{C. allenii}’s leaves simply wither and die without reabsorbing substantial nitrogen or chlorophyll (Figure \ref{fig:sup_trait_distributions}). We quantified nitrogen and chlorophyll reabsorption during senescence in the mapping population as a proxy for drought deciduousness. We measured both leaf chlorophyll and nitrogen content before and after drought-induced leaf death. Mapping population hybrids grew for eight weeks in 1-gallon pots before we began the drought treatment. On the first day of no water, we recorded the average of three Soil Plant Analysis Development(SPAD; Minolta Camera Co., Osaka, Japan) meter readings of chlorophyll content taken near the tip, middle, and base of the oldest healthy leaf blade. From the same leaf, we also collected six hole punches of leaf tissue from the same locations as the SPAD readings, but on both halves of the leaf (one hole punch on each half of the leaf at the tip, middle, and base). We repeated the chlorophyll content measures and leaf hole punches when the leaf and the younger leaf above it were both fully dead. We dried all leaf tissue immediately after collection at  60°C for 72 hours and stored and shipped in silica. Leaf nitrogen concentration was determined with an elemental analyzer at Chapman University and at the UC Davis Stable Isotope facility. We quantified chlorophyll and nitrogen reabsorption as:
\[
1 - \frac{\text{dead leaf value}}{\text{green leaf value}} \times 100
= \%\,\text{leaf nutrient reabsorption}
\]

\paragraph{Leaf thickness and toughness}
Leaf toughness and thickness are associated with herbivore defense and resource use strategy. To measure leaf thickness, we used a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo IP65, Global Industrial, NY, USA) to take measurements at three points on the 4th fully expanded leaf from the top of the stem. We took the average of measurements from the center of the leaf ca. 1 cm from the leaf edge, midway between the edge of the leaf and the midvein, and ca. 1 cm from the midvein. To measure leaf toughness, we used a Medio-Line 40300, 300 g max penetrometer (Pesola AG, Switzerland), to measure the force (g) required to puncture a 1 mm diameter hole in the leaf. We placed each leaf between two sheets of plexiglass with a 5 mm hole through both and centered the penetrometer rod in the hole, maintaining the leaf at a constant tension. We took the average of toughness measures collected at roughly the same three points as measured for thickness, transecting the middle of the leaf.

\subsubsection{Local ancestry assignment}
  
We mapped local ancestry across the genome for each individual in the F3 mapping population using Ancestry HMM \citep{corbett2017hidden} and following the same methods as for the wild hybrids.

\subsubsection{Linkage mapping}
To identify and account for structural differences between our reference genome and our focal species, and to get marker distance estimates in centimorgans (cMs), we generated linkage maps with Lep-MAP3 \citep{rastas2017lep}) using sequence data from the original wild F1, 21 F2s generated by selfing the F1, and the 389 F3s generated by selfing 5 of the F2s. We converted the genotype probabilities calculated with aHMM (above) into hard calls, keeping only sites with a call of greater than 90\% probability. We incorporated the pedigree into the call file with \texttt{ParentCall2}. We used \texttt{SeparateChromosomes2} to assign markers to linkage groups, specifying a LOD limit of 11 and a theta of 0.001, and setting `distortionLod` to 11 to filter for segregation distortion. To order markers within linkage groups we used \texttt{OrderMarkers2}, then plotted the cM position vs the reference bp position. We found that chromosome 4 was split into 3 linkage groups, likely due to a lack of informative recombination in our mapping population in large regions near the centromere; we manually joined these linkage groups. We also found and removed 2 small translocations in centromere regions (on chromosomes 2 \& 5) that could not be confidently placed. These modifications left 9 linkage groups corresponding to the 9 chromosomes. We re-ran \texttt{OrderMarkers2} 100 times for each chromosome and selected the run with the highest likelihood to use for cM position estimates.  

\subsubsection{QTL mapping}
To identify regions of the genome associated with traits important for habitat isolation between these species, we performed QTL mapping with R/qtl \citep{broman_rqtl_2003}. We used the markers and genotype calls with cM position from Lep-MAP3 described above. We dropped one individual with genotypes at less than 80\% of markers, and removed markers found in fewer than 80\% of genotyped individuals. We then thinned uninformative markers with \texttt{findDupMarkers}, leaving 14,889 markers with an average of 97.5\% of individuals genotyped at any given marker. We also identified and removed 3 individuals with identical pairs. This left a total of 385 individuals with genotype data. We calculated genotype probabilities with the \texttt{calc.genoprob} function, and the recombination frequencies with \texttt{est.rf}. While most phenotype data was roughly normally distributed, seed dormancy had a long tail generated by unusually long dormancy times; we log-transformed seed dormancy to improve normality before model selection and fitting.

To determine appropriate covariates to include in the models for associating genotypes and phenotypes, we first used the R \texttt{step} function for stepwise selection via AIC, both through forward and backward elimination. We included hybrid index (percent \textit{C. villosissimus} ancestry), F2 families, and cohorts as potential covariates. Cohort was the same for all phenotype measurements other than seed dormancy, as seeds germinating in the same week were part of the same measurement cohort. For seed dormancy, cohort was defined by the time of seed collection and sowing. For each phenotype, we retained all covariates with a significant effect, using the 'one-hot encoding' method to recode cohort and family variables so they are treated categorically by R/qtl. This enabled us to avoid over-fitting by dropping from the models specific cohorts and families that were not significant covariates. 

We then performed a genome-wide scan using a single QTL model with R/qtl’s \texttt{scanone} function with the Haley-Knott regression method \citep{haley_simple_1992}) to scan for associations between phenotypes and genotypes at ancestry-informative markers. We estimated and plotted 10\%  LOD (logarithm of odds) false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds for each phenotype with 1,000 permutations (\ref{fig:sup_scanone}). We used \texttt{fitqtl} to fit an initial model with the same covariates determined above and including the QTL peak or peaks that were above the FDR threshold. 

To find the best model for all contributing QTL and their potential interactions, we ran \texttt{stepwiseqtl}, R/qtl's automated function for forward/backward multiple QTL model selection, with penalties on main effects and interactions calculated with \texttt{calc.penalties} from 1000 permutations of \texttt{scantwo} for a 0.05 alpha. For computational tractability, we reduced the marker set with another, more stringent round of duplicate removal, and calculated genotype probabilities at 5 cM steps with an error probability of 0.01. We then ran \texttt{fitqtl} on the original, larger cross object with genotype probabilities calculated at marker locations using the model and QTL peak positions selected by \texttt{stepwiseqtl} after running \texttt{refineqtl} on the peak positions with the original cross object. To determine the size of the QTL windows, we used R/qtl's \texttt{bayesint} function to calculate 95\% Bayes credible intervals. 

We also note that iterating \texttt{refineqtl} to refine QTL peak positions, \texttt{addqtl} to add additional peaks one at a time, and \texttt{fitqtl} to check for significance of the additional peaks identifies multiple additional peaks for some phenotypes. Here, we conservatively only report results from the model identified via a penalized LOD score with \texttt{stepwiseqtl}, other than for growth rate and percent chlorophyll reabsorbed during drought. For growth rate, 1000 permutations of scanone identified a peak on chromosome 3 that was not included in the model from \texttt{stepwiseqtl}, but that is significant at a p-value of <0.01 when included in the model; we report results from this model. For percent chlorophyll reabsorbed during drought, \texttt{stepwiseqtl} erroneously identified two peaks within one broad peak on chromosome 1. The two loci identified have zero recombination events between them in our mapping population, and thus are part of one QTL; we report results from modeling a single QTL. 

\subsection{Relating introgression to the genetics of ecogeographic isolation}
To investigate patterns of introgression and differentiation around the QTL loci that contribute to ecogeographic isolation, we compared genome-wide \(F_{st}\) to the average \(F_{st}\) within QTL windows. For each chromosome, we plotted the QTL peaks and credible intervals over \(F_{st}\) calculated in 10kb non-overlapping windows with 99th percentile outliers highlighted in red (Figures \ref{fig:3_undiff_QTL} and \ref{fig:sup_genomewide_Fst_QTL_CIs}). Next, we calculated windowed genome-wide \(F_{st}\) averages and compared them with QTL window averages. We first estimated cM positions for the summary statistic windows by fitting isotonic regressions to each chromosome using the cM and bp marker positions from Lep-MAP3. We excluded chromosome 4 from this analysis due to poor genetic mapping. We then averaged \(F_{st}\) in one cM windows across the genome and for each QTL window that was less than 15 cM (~10 Mbp). This excluded the windows for nitrogen reabsorption during drought on chromosome 2 and the for growth rate on chromosome 5, which had CIs larger than 15 cM. Finally, we compared the distribution of genome-wide windowed \(F_{st}\) values to QTL window averages (Figure \ref{fig:3_undiff_QTL}D).
